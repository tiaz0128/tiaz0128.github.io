## Sprint?

Right after PyCon ended, I got an email inviting me to the [sprint](https://event-us.kr/pythonkorea/event/94151){:target="_blank"} the following weekend.

### What's a Sprint?

> A sprint is an event where people who want to contribute to open-source projects gather in one place and develop intensively. It's a great opportunity to meet new peers, share common interests, and contribute to the open-source ecosystem together.

When I first got the email, the words that jumped out were "open source" and "contribute." I'd only ever used open source; I had no idea how to actually contribute and zero experience doing it. So naturally, my first thought was:

"But... can I actually do this?"

![Open source is scary](/assets/img/content/insight/002/001.webp){:.img-s}

`> Contributing to open source...? What's that... sounds scary...`{:.img-caption}

## The Pre-Leader Program

The email described two main programs.

- Sprint : CPython / Donghee Na (Python Core Developer, sprint leader)
- Sprint Pre-Leader Program /  Kwonhan Bae (Python Software Foundation board member)

The "Pre-Leader Program" said it was for **people who aren't sure how to contribute**{:.orange} — it felt like it was saying, "Yes, you. You right there."

### Sprint Pre-Leader Program

> A program **for people who aren't sure how to contribute**. In particular, for those who want to contribute to projects without a maintainer in Korea, it offers a chance to figure out together how to contribute on your own. You can join without worrying even if you don't know how to contribute; it's a meta-style program where we think it through and find the way together.

"Oh? So they'll help me figure it out? OK, let's sign up."

## Any Open Source You're Interested In?

The sign-up email asked us to list any open-source projects we were interested in. The first thing that came to mind was [an open-source project I use a lot but that's missing a feature I want](https://github.com/mingrammer/diagrams){:.none target="_blank"}. I wrote that one down. Then I hurriedly did some reading on open-source contribution.

"Tens of thousands of stars, contributing to popular open source... contributor!"

Yes, that's the stuff! I figured I'd look pretty impressive if I landed something in [an open-source project everybody knows](https://github.com/pydantic/pydantic){:.none target="_blank"}, so I added one more with that mindset.

## Contribution Is Not a Career Move

That Saturday, I went to the sprint and sat there awkwardly again. First, they talked about what contributing actually means. But the very first thing they said made me flinch a little.

["Contribution is not a career move."](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1zhtmcAeEqmoO98XJazFEb3Gh0CQa_z61ncyBCUKkHoI/edit?usp=sharing){:target="_blank"}

Starting from that, they explained that everyone in open source is a volunteer, and that we tend to think of contributing as some grand thing and end up hardly contributing at all. **It's fine to start with the easy stuff.**{:.orange}

## Many Ways to Contribute: Does This Count?

Even the things that make you go, "Does something this simple and trivial really count as a contribution?" — the answer is yes, they all count.

It was great that they shared contribution methods from their own experience and helped each participant find ways to contribute to the open-source projects they cared about!

- Sponsoring
- Fixing typos in docs / translation
- Reporting / fixing bugs
- Adding features, and more

Honestly, I heard and learned so much that I can't even write it all down. Anyway, the key point is that contribution starts with very small things. Just give it a try!

## The Contribution Mindset

The "Pre-Leader" sprint program I joined didn't have an open-source maintainer present. A real sprint, I think, is a rare synchronous experience where you can open a PR straight to the maintainer and get feedback on the spot.

But most of the open-source contribution we experience seems to be **asynchronous**. When you want a review, the maintainer might be spending time with their family.

__In the end, everything is done by people, so you need to be considerate and patient.__{:.orange} If anything, I came to think that's actually a good thing. You get to think twice before you act, and you have time to run your shaky English through a translator and look it over again!

## Wrapping Up

Before the sprint, my mindset was "Can I actually do this?" After it ended, it had changed to **"Contributing to open source? Yeah, I can do this too."**{:.orange} For me, the sprint was a wonderful experience that brought open source a lot closer!

And thanks to it, I started contributing to open source too. 😊

- Sponsoring the [Python Software Foundation](https://github.com/python){:target="_blank"}
- Sponsoring the [Python Korea User Group](https://github.com/pythonkr){:target="_blank"}
- Sponsoring a maintainer (buymeacoffee)
- Saying I wanted to contribute a feature I needed, and getting a positive reply
- Making PRs, starting with the easy things I can do!

Finally, it was a shame that fewer people came to the sprint than I expected. It's a fantastic experience, so I really hope you'll join one too!

## Afterword

### 2024-11-02

First, I sent the maintainer [a cup of coffee](https://buymeacoffee.com/mingrammer){:target="_blank"} and talked with them about a PR, and they actually confirmed the feature I wanted!

### 2025-02-04

After a few attempts, a really simple [PR got approved!](https://github.com/mingrammer/diagrams/pull/1093){:target="_blank"} My first open-source contribution.😊 It took about four months from my first PR.

Starting out was scary, but I learned that truly anyone can do it and that everyone is kind. I plan to keep contributing to open source. Thank you!
